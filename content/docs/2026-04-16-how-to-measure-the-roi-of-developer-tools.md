  on April 15, 2026 by Arsh Sharma | CNCF Ambassador, Senior Dev Rel at MetalBear
  CNCF projects highlighted in this post There’s been a growing emphasis in the cloud
  native community on investing in tools that improve developer experience. Platform
  engineering, accompanied with the rise of projects like Backstage, is all about
  making developers more productive by smoothing out the day-to-day friction in how
  they build and ship software. The CNCF TAG App Delivery’s Platform Engineering Whitepaper
  documents how organizations are formalizing this practice, and with that formalization
  comes increasing pressure to demonstrate that the investment pays off. Whether you’re
  adopting a paid product or a free open source project, developer tools always come
  with a cost. Teams need to spend time evaluating them, integrating them into existing
  workflows, and maintaining them over time. Without a clear way to think about ROI,
  it can be difficult to justify these investments beyond a general sense that they
  “improve developer experience. ” As someone who’s worked in the developer tools
  space for over four years now, this is a question I’ve had to answer many times
  with customers. In this post, I’ll start by looking at some of the common ways teams
  measure the ROI of developer tools, and then cover how those approaches apply differently
  depending on team size, and which ones tend to be useful at each stage of growth.
  Internal surveys and direct feedback are often the simplest way for teams to understand
  whether a developer tool is providing real value or not. They’re easy to run and
  don’t require you to set up complex systems to gather or study a lot of data. This
  approach relies primarily on qualitative feedback, which is often undervalued when
  it comes to ROI calculations compared to quantitative metrics. Numbers feel objective
  and easier to report on, so they tend to win by default.
